TIPS — Readability Scores

The NLP Suite computes five standard readability indices for each document in your corpus. These formulas estimate the reading difficulty of a text, typically expressed as a US school grade level.

=== The Five Measures ===

1. FLESCH READING EASE (Rudolf Flesch, 1948)

   Formula: 206.835 - 1.015 * (total words / total sentences) - 84.6 * (total syllables / total words)

   Score range: 0 to ~120 (higher = easier to read)

   Interpretation:
      100-90  Very Easy        (5th grade)
       90-80  Easy             (6th grade)
       80-70  Fairly Easy      (7th grade)
       70-60  Standard         (8th-9th grade)
       60-50  Fairly Difficult (10th-12th grade)
       50-30  Difficult        (College level)
       30-0   Very Difficult   (Graduate level)

2. FLESCH-KINCAID GRADE LEVEL (J. Peter Kincaid, 1975)

   Formula: 0.39 * (total words / total sentences) + 11.8 * (total syllables / total words) - 15.59

   Output: US school grade level (e.g., 8.2 means an 8th-grader can understand the text)

3. GUNNING FOG INDEX (Robert Gunning, 1952)

   Formula: 0.4 * [(total words / total sentences) + 100 * (polysyllabic words / total words)]

   Output: Years of formal education needed. A Fog Index of 12 requires high-school-senior reading level.
   Polysyllabic = 3 or more syllables.

4. COLEMAN-LIAU INDEX (Meri Coleman & T.L. Liau, 1975)

   Formula: 0.0588 * L - 0.296 * S - 15.8
   where L = average number of characters per 100 words, S = average number of sentences per 100 words

   Unlike the other formulas, Coleman-Liau uses character counts instead of syllable counts, making it easier to compute automatically.

5. AUTOMATED READABILITY INDEX (ARI) (Senter & Smith, 1967)

   Formula: 4.71 * (characters / words) + 0.5 * (words / sentences) - 21.43

   Like Coleman-Liau, ARI avoids syllable counting and uses character counts instead.


=== Why Scores Can Be Extreme ===

These formulas were calibrated in the 1940s-1970s for typical English prose (newspapers, textbooks, government documents). They can produce unexpected values for edge cases:

NEGATIVE OR ZERO SCORES:
- Flesch Reading Ease goes negative when texts have very long sentences with many polysyllabic words. Academic papers, legal documents, and scientific writing routinely produce raw scores below zero.
- Flesch-Kincaid Grade can go negative for extremely short, simple texts (e.g., single-word sentences like "Stop. Go. Run.").
- The NLP Suite clamps all scores to sensible ranges: Flesch Reading Ease to 0-121, and all grade-level indices to 0-30. A score of 0 may indicate the raw formula produced a negative value.

VERY HIGH SCORES:
- Gunning Fog is especially sensitive to technical vocabulary. Scientific writing with terms like "electromagnetic" or "photosynthesis" (3+ syllables) produces Fog scores above 20, even when the prose is clear.
- A Fog score of 20+ does not necessarily mean the text is badly written — it means it contains specialized vocabulary.

SYLLABLE COUNTING LIMITATIONS:
- The NLP Suite estimates syllable counts using a regex-based heuristic (counting vowel groups). This is approximately 85-90% accurate for English. Proper names, abbreviations, and non-English words may be miscounted.
- Coleman-Liau and ARI avoid this issue entirely by using character counts.


=== Interpreting Results Across Documents ===

Readability scores are most useful for COMPARING documents within a corpus, not for absolute judgments. If Document A has a Flesch-Kincaid Grade of 12 and Document B has a grade of 8, Document B is meaningfully simpler — even if you disagree with the specific grade number.

For corpus-level analysis, look at:
- The RANGE of scores across documents (are some documents much harder than others?)
- The TREND if documents are ordered chronologically (does the writing get simpler or more complex over time?)
- OUTLIERS — documents with scores far from the corpus mean may warrant closer inspection.
